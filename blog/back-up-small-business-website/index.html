How to back up a small business website (and know it will actually restore)

Knowing how to back up a small business website means knowing three things: what to save, how often to save it, and how to prove you can get it back. Here is each one, in order.

By Harris, Avello Labs · November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

In short

  • Back up two things: your site's files and its database (the part that stores your pages, forms and orders).
  • Back up as often as your site changes. A site that rarely changes needs fewer backups than one that takes orders or bookings.
  • Keep copies somewhere other than your web host, and test a restore. A backup you have never restored is a guess.

Why a backup matters more than you think

Most small business sites run fine for years. Then one day an update breaks something, a plugin (an add-on that gives your site extra features) conflicts with another, someone's login gets stolen, or a host has an outage. If you have a good backup, that is a bad afternoon. If you don't, it can mean rebuilding pages from memory.

Picture a plumber in Camden County whose contact form stops working after an update. Or a salon in Burlington County whose booking page gets overwritten by mistake. In both cases the fix is the same: roll the site back to the last good copy. That only works if the copy exists and is recent.

What to back up

A website is usually made of two parts. You need both.

  • Files. Your theme or design, uploaded photos, PDFs, plugins, and settings files. These are the pieces you can see and click on.
  • The database. This is where your text, page content, blog posts, form entries, customer accounts and orders live. If your site is built on a system like WordPress, almost everything you have typed into it sits here.

Many people back up only the files, or only the database, and find out too late. A files-only copy gives you a site with no content. A database-only copy gives you content with no design.

Also write down the things that are not part of the site itself but that you will need to bring it back:

  • Your domain name registrar login (the company where you bought your web address).
  • Your DNS settings. DNS is the address book that tells the internet where your site lives.
  • Your hosting login.
  • Any email service tied to your domain.
  • Logins for tools connected to the site, such as booking, payments or your email list.

Keep these in a password manager or a locked note, not in a sticky note on the monitor.

How often to back up

The rule is simple: back up as often as you can afford to lose work.

Ask yourself what you would lose if the site went back to its last backup. Then pick a schedule that matches.

  • A mostly static site (a brochure site for a law firm or HVAC company that changes a few times a year): a regular automatic backup plus one before every change is plenty.
  • A site that changes often (a restaurant that updates menus, a contractor posting new projects): backups should run on a regular schedule, and you should take one right before any edit.
  • A site that takes orders, bookings or form leads: this is where you want the most frequent backups. New data arrives all day. If the site goes back to last night's copy, today's orders are gone.

Always take a fresh backup before you do any of these:

  1. Update the site's software or plugins.
  2. Change the design or theme.
  3. Move to a new host.
  4. Let someone new, such as a freelancer, make changes.

Many hosts include automatic backups. That is a good start, but read what they cover and how long they keep copies. Hosts differ, and a plan's terms can change, so check your own account instead of assuming.

Where to keep your backups

Do not keep your only backup in the same place as your site. If the host has a problem, or your account is locked or hacked, both the site and the backup can disappear together.

A sensible setup:

  • One copy with your host, so a quick restore is easy.
  • One copy somewhere separate, such as a cloud storage account you control.
  • Access protected with a strong, unique password and two-step login.

Make sure the backup is stored under your account, not only your web person's. If the relationship ends, you should still be able to reach your own files.

How to test that a restore works

This is the step almost everyone skips. A backup that cannot be restored is only a folder of files that makes you feel safe.

Test it like this:

  1. Make a test copy of your site. Ask your host or web person for a staging site. That is a private duplicate of your site where you can experiment without touching the live one.
  2. Restore your latest backup onto the test copy. Do not restore over your live site for a test.
  3. Click through it. Open the home page, a few service pages, and your contact form. Send a test message and check that it arrives.
  4. Check the newest content. Look for something you added recently, like a new blog post or a changed phone number. If it is missing, your backup is older than you think.
  5. Check the extras. Make sure images load, the booking tool works, and the address and hours are right.
  6. Write down how long it took and who did it. If the answer is "nobody knows how," you have found a gap worth fixing now.

Do this at least a couple of times a year, and again after any big change to your site or hosting. Set a calendar reminder so it does not slip.

Signs your backup plan has a hole

  • You cannot say where your backups are stored.
  • Nobody has ever restored one.
  • The only copy is on your host's server.
  • The backup was set up years ago and nobody has looked at it since.
  • Your site collects form leads or orders, but backups run rarely.

If two or more of those sound familiar, fix that first. Backups also fit into a bigger routine. Doing it yourself or having someone handle it

You can set up backups yourself if your host offers a simple tool and you are comfortable following steps. The hard part is not the setup. It is remembering to check it and test it.

Common questions

Is my web host's backup enough?

It is a good first layer, but not enough alone. If the backup lives only on your host's server, a host problem or locked account can take both away. Keep a second copy somewhere separate that you control.

Do I need to back up a simple five-page brochure site?

Yes. It may change rarely, but rebuilding the text, photos and settings from scratch takes real time. A backup before each change and a regular automatic copy is usually enough.

What is the difference between a backup and a restore?

A backup is the saved copy. A restore is the act of putting that copy back to bring your site back. You need both to work, which is why testing a restore matters.

Can a backup bring back a hacked site?

It can, if you restore a copy from before the problem began. You also need to fix how the attacker got in, such as an outdated plugin or a weak password, or it can happen again.

Who should have access to the backups?

You should, as the business owner, even if someone else manages them. Keep the logins in a password manager and share access only with people you trust.
