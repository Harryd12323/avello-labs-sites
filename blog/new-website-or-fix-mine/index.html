Do I need a new website or can mine be fixed?

Do I need a new website or can mine be fixed? Most small business sites fall clearly into one camp, and a few simple checks will tell you which.

By Avello Labs · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Fix it when the problems are on the surface: old text, a slow image, a broken form, a missing page.
  • Rebuild it when the problems are in the foundation: it breaks on phones, can't be edited, or is stuck on tools nobody supports.
  • Ask one question: is the site's structure sound, or are you patching the same thing again and again?
  • A free check can sort this out in minutes, before you spend anything.

Start with what you actually want the site to do

Before you look at the site, decide what it is for. For most local businesses the answer is simple. A customer finds you, trusts you, and calls, books or fills out a form.

A site that does that job, even if it looks a little dated, usually deserves repair. A site that can't do that job, no matter how many tweaks you make, needs a rebuild.

Think of a plumber in Camden County. If the phone number is wrong, the photos are old, and the service list is missing a service, that is a fix. If the site falls apart on a phone and the owner can't change a word without calling a stranger, that is a different problem.

Signs your website can be fixed

These are problems that sit on top of a decent foundation. Each one can be repaired without starting over.

  • The information is out of date. Old hours, an old team photo, a service you no longer offer. Updating text and photos is cheap work, and it matters. See how often you should update your website.
  • One page is slow because of big images. Oversized photos are a common cause. Shrinking them often helps a lot.
  • A form or button is broken. A contact form that doesn't send, or a phone number that isn't tappable on a phone. These are small repairs.
  • The site works on a phone, it just looks plain. If the layout holds together on a small screen, the structure is probably fine.
  • Your security notice says "Not secure." This is usually a missing or expired certificate, which is a small job. Here is what an SSL certificate is.
  • You get visitors but few calls. That is often a wording, layout or trust problem, not a sign the whole site is bad. We cover it in website getting traffic but no calls.
  • Google can find you, but your listing is thin. Better page titles, a clearer description and a stronger Google Business Profile can help without touching the design.

If most of your problems are on this list, fix the site. You will save money and keep what already works.

Signs it's time to rebuild

These problems live in the foundation. Patching them is like repainting a house with a cracked frame.

  • It doesn't work properly on a phone. Text is tiny, buttons overlap, menus don't open. Many of your customers search from a phone, often while standing in a driveway or sitting in a parking lot.
  • You can't edit it yourself, and nobody can edit it for you. Maybe the person who built it is gone. Maybe it was made in a tool that was shut down. If every small change turns into a project, the setup is working against you.
  • Updates keep breaking things. You fix one problem and two more appear. That usually means the site is built on layers of old add-ons that no longer fit together.
  • It has been hacked more than once. A repeat problem points to old software that can't be made safe. A one-time hack that was cleaned up properly is different.
  • The pages are a maze. Some sites grew by adding a page here and a page there for years. Customers can't find what they need, and neither can search engines.
  • Your business changed a lot. New services, new locations, a new name. If the site was built for the old business, bending it to fit the new one can cost more than starting clean.
  • The cost of fixing is close to the cost of replacing. If a repair quote sounds as large as a rebuild, take that as a hint. For rough budgeting, see how much a small business website costs.

If several of these sound familiar, a rebuild is likely the better choice.

A simple test: the third patch rule

Here is a rule we use. Think about the last few times you paid for or did a repair on the site.

  1. Was it the same kind of problem each time? If yes, the cause is deeper than the repair.
  2. Did the repair last? If it keeps coming back, the foundation is the issue.
  3. Does the site still do what you want after the repair? If not, you are fixing the wrong thing.

If you are on your third patch for the same problem, stop patching. Rebuild.

What to keep when you rebuild

A rebuild doesn't mean throwing away everything. Good rebuilds keep the parts that earn their place.

  • Your address. If your site has been around for a while, search engines and other sites already know it. Keep the same web address (domain) where you can.
  • Your good pages. If a page already brings people in, keep its content and its address, or set up a redirect. A redirect is a signpost that sends visitors from the old address to the new one, so nobody lands on an error page.
  • Your words and photos. Real photos of your work and honest descriptions of your services are worth carrying over.
  • Your Google Business Profile. It is separate from your website and keeps working. Here is why your Google Business Profile matters.

Ask anyone you hire how they plan to handle old addresses. If they have no answer, that is a warning sign.

Don't forget how people find you now

More people now ask AI tools like ChatGPT for local recommendations. That is called AEO, short for answer engine optimization. Those tools need to read your site clearly, which a messy or broken site can make harder. Whether you fix or rebuild, make sure the basics are clear: what you do, where you work, and how to reach you. You can learn more in what AEO is and why SEO matters for local businesses.

Nobody can promise that a new site will get you mentioned or ranked. A clear, working site just gives you a fair chance.

What we would do first

If you aren't sure, don't guess. Do this in order.

  1. Open your site on your own phone. Try to call, book or send a message.
  2. List the problems you see. Mark each as surface or foundation using the lists above.
  3. Ask whoever built it whether they can still edit it, and how.
  4. Run the free site check to see what it finds.
  5. Decide. Mostly surface problems, fix. Mostly foundation problems, rebuild.

Common questions

Is an old-looking website a reason to rebuild?

Not by itself. If it works on phones, loads reasonably and gets people to contact you, a fresh coat of design may be all you need. Look at how it works before you look at how it looks.

Will a new website hurt my Google presence?

It can if old addresses are dropped or the content is thrown away. Careful planning, including redirects from old pages to new ones, lowers that risk. Nobody can promise exactly how search engines will react.

How long does a rebuild take?

It depends on the size of the site and how fast you send content and photos. Many small business sites can go live within a week with us, but larger sites take longer.

Can I fix it myself?

Small things, yes. Updating hours, swapping photos and fixing a phone number are often easy if you can log in. If you can't log in, or fixes keep breaking other things, get help.
