Website getting traffic but no calls? Here's why, and how to fix it

If your website is getting traffic but no calls, the problem is usually not the number of visitors. It's what they see in the first few seconds after they land.

By Avello Labs · October 26, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Visitors who don't call usually can't find your number, don't know what to do next, or give up on a slow page.
  • Most fixes are small: a bigger phone button, one clear next step, lighter images, and plain descriptions of what you do.
  • Check your site on your own phone first. That one test shows you most of the problem.

First, confirm the problem is real

"Traffic" can mean many things. Before you change anything, look at who is actually visiting.

Open your analytics, or ask whoever manages your site to. You want to know which pages people land on, and what device they use. If most visitors are on phones, then your phone experience is your website. If a lot of the traffic goes to a blog post and nowhere else, those readers may never have intended to hire anyone.

Also check that calls aren't happening and going untracked. A plumber in Burlington County might get calls that come straight from the Google listing, not the site. That's fine, but it means the site may not be the weak spot.

Then do the test that matters most. Pick up your phone, stand somewhere with ordinary signal, and pretend you're a stranger with a leaking pipe. Search for your business, open your site, and try to call. What you notice in that minute is worth more than any report.

Reason 1: The phone number is buried

This is the most common one, and the easiest to fix. The number sits in the footer, or inside a paragraph, or only on a contact page three taps deep.

A person with an urgent problem will not hunt. They go back and tap the next result.

How to fix it:

  1. Put your phone number at the top of every page, in large type.
  2. On phones, make it a tap-to-call button. Technically this is a "tel link," a bit of code that tells the phone to start a call when tapped. If your number is plain text, people have to copy it by hand.
  3. Keep the number the same everywhere, on the site and on your Google listing.
  4. Keep it visible while people scroll, if your design allows it. A small bar at the bottom of the screen on phones works well for HVAC companies and emergency trades.

Reason 2: There's no clear next step

Some sites show a lot of pages and never say what the visitor should do. Others offer five different things at once: call, email, fill out a form, follow us, read the blog.

Too many choices means no choice. Pick the one action you want most, and make it obvious.

For a dentist, that might be "Book an appointment." For a roofing contractor, "Get a free estimate." For a restaurant, "Call to order" or "Reserve a table." Put that same action at the top of the page, after each major section, and at the very bottom.

A few rules help:

  • Use a button, not a text link buried in a sentence.
  • Write the label as what the visitor gets, like "Get my estimate," not "Submit."
  • If you have a form, ask for only what you need. Name, phone, and a short note is often enough. Every extra field loses some people.
  • Say what happens next. "We call you back the same business day" is the kind of line that makes a person hit the button, as long as it's true.

Reason 3: The site is slow on phones

A site can look fine on your office computer and crawl on a phone. Large photos are the usual cause. A contractor uploads full-size pictures straight from a camera, and the phone has to download all of them before the page appears.

Google pays attention to this too. Its Search Central documentation describes Core Web Vitals as measures of loading, interactivity and visual stability, meaning how fast the page appears, how soon it responds to taps, and whether things jump around while loading. You don't need to learn the jargon. You need pages that appear quickly and hold still.

How to fix it:

  • Shrink and compress your photos before uploading, or have your site do it automatically.
  • Remove things you don't need, such as extra video players, old pop-ups, and plugins you stopped using.
  • Show only a few photos on the home page and put the full gallery on its own page.
  • Test on a real phone using mobile data, not only on your office wifi.

Speed is also a build question. A site built lean from the start is easier to keep fast than one that has had years of add-ons. It's part of why we build sites to go live quickly and keep them light, and why ongoing website care matters. Sites slow down as things get added over time.

Reason 4: Visitors can't tell what you do

Read your home page as if you've never heard of your business. Within a few seconds, can you tell what you do, where you do it, and who it's for?

Vague headlines cause real damage. "Quality service you can trust" could be a law firm, a landscaper, or a salon. A visitor who has to work that out will leave.

How to fix it:

  • Make the top headline say the service and the area. "Emergency plumbing in Camden and Gloucester counties" beats a slogan.
  • List your main services on the home page, each with a short description and a link to its own page.
  • Give each service its own page. A med spa that offers several treatments should not squeeze them all into one paragraph.
  • Say who you're best for, and who you're not. It saves everyone time.
  • Show a few real photos of your work, your shop, or your staff. Stock photos make people doubt you.

This also helps you get found. Pages that clearly say what you do are easier for search engines and AI tools to understand. If you're curious about that side, our post on why your business might not be showing up on Google covers it.

Reason 5: Trust is missing at the moment of decision

Someone is about to call a stranger. They want a reason to feel safe.

Check that your site shows:

  • Your real business name, address, and service area
  • Your license or insurance details, if your trade needs them
  • Reviews or quotes from real customers, with permission
  • Clear hours, so people know when you'll answer
  • A page about who you are, with real names and faces

Don't invent anything. If you have no reviews on the site yet, link to the ones on your Google Business Profile or paste a few in with the customer's permission.

A simple order to work in

You don't need to fix everything at once. Go in this order:

  1. Make the phone number big and tap-to-call on every page.
  2. Choose one main action and repeat it.
  3. Fix the headline so a stranger knows what you do.
  4. Compress your photos and retest on a phone.
  5. Add trust details near your buttons.

After each change, wait a reasonable period and watch whether calls change. Change one thing at a time if you can, so you know what helped. Nobody can promise a specific result, but these are the fixes that most often matter.

Common questions

How long should I wait to see if my changes work?

It depends on how much traffic you get. A busy site shows a pattern sooner than a quiet one. Look at a few weeks of calls before and after, and avoid judging from a single day.

Is it a problem if my traffic is mostly from blog posts?

Not necessarily, but blog readers are often looking for information, not a contractor. Add a clear next step to each post, such as a button to call or request an estimate, and link to the page for the service the post is about.

Should I add a pop-up asking people to call?

Usually not. Pop-ups can annoy visitors on phones, and Google has long treated intrusive pop-ups on mobile as a page experience issue. A clear button in the page works better.

Could my site be fine and the problem be something else?

Yes. Wrong kind of traffic is common, such as people outside your service area. Check which searches bring people in, and whether your Google listing and website say the same thing.
