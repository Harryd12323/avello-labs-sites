Robots.txt basics: what a local business owner should check

Search Engine Journal just published a robots.txt guide for search and AI crawlers. Here is what robots.txt for small business sites comes down to.

News · By Harris, Avello Labs · November 20, 2026 · 3 min read

In short

  • Robots.txt is a small text file that tells bots which parts of your site they may visit.
  • Blocking the wrong bot can hide your business from search results or AI tools.
  • Most local sites need very little in this file, but a mistake in it can do real harm.
  • A quick check now can confirm you are not blocking anyone by accident.

What happened

Search Engine Journal reports on a new guide to robots.txt, the plain text file that sits at the top level of your website. It tells crawlers, the automated programs that visit sites for search engines and AI tools, which pages they should leave alone.

The guide's point is that more kinds of bots now visit websites. Along with traditional search crawlers, AI search tools and agents come by too, and each follows its own rules. Block the wrong one and your content can drop out of search results or out of AI answers.

The guide also walks through how rules are read. A bot with its own named section follows that section instead of the general one. When two rules conflict, Google applies the more specific one. It then shows examples of what sites commonly block: internal search pages, filter and sort links, shopping cart links, account areas and some PDFs. It also notes that robots.txt asks bots to stay out. It does not hide private information and does not guarantee a page vanishes from search.

What it means for a local business

Most of that advice is written for big online stores with endless filtered pages. A plumber or a salon with a ten-page site does not have that problem.

The risk for you is different. A single stray line can block everything. Say a web designer built your dental practice site on a test address and blocked all bots while it was being built. If that line was carried over when the site went live, search engines are told to stay out. Nobody notices until the phone gets quiet.

The AI side matters too. If you want to be recommended by tools like ChatGPT, you do not want to turn their crawlers away without meaning to. Some owners do choose to block bots that collect content for AI training, and that is a fair choice. The point is to make it on purpose, not by accident. One more catch from the guide: a rule with a missing trailing slash can block more pages than you intended.

What to do about it

  1. Open yourwebsite.com/robots.txt in your browser. Most sites have one, and it is short.
  2. Look for a line that disallows everything, written as a single slash. On a live business site, that is almost always a mistake.
  3. Check that no search or AI crawler is blocked that you want visiting. Our AI crawler check shows this, and our post on checking if your site blocks AI crawlers explains it.
  4. If you want a page kept out of search, do not rely on robots.txt alone. Ask your web person about a "noindex" tag, which tells search engines not to list a page.
  5. Keep private files behind a login. Robots.txt is a request, not a lock.
  6. After any redesign or move, check this file again. That is part of what we do in website care, and our AEO work covers how AI tools find you.
