How long does it take to build a small business website?

How long does it take to build a small business website? The building is rarely the slow part. The waiting is, and most of it is in your hands.

By Harris, Avello Labs · November 21, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • The honest answer is "it depends," and what it depends on is mostly content, feedback and decisions, not code.
  • Most delays come from missing photos and text, slow rounds of feedback, and too many people signing off.
  • You can keep a project moving by gathering your material first, naming one decision maker, and answering quickly.
  • At Avello Labs, most sites go live within a week, and that works best when the owner is ready.

The short answer

Ask ten web designers how long a site takes and you will hear ten answers. Some say a few weeks. Some say a few months. Both can be true, because the number is not really about how hard the site is to build.

A typical small business site is a home page, a services page or two, an about page and a contact page. A designer can put that together fairly quickly. What stretches the schedule is everything that has to happen around the build.

Think of it like a kitchen remodel. The cabinets go in fast. The weeks disappear while you wait on the countertop choice, the tile that is on backorder, or a change of mind about the layout. Websites work the same way.

For us, most sites go live within a week. That is how we usually work, not a promise for every project. It holds up when the content is ready and the answers come back quickly. When they don't, the clock stretches, and that is true at any studio.

What actually slows a website project down

Here are the four things we see most often.

1. Gathering content

This is the biggest one. A site needs words, photos and facts, and small business owners are busy running the business.

Common holdups:

  • No decent photos of the work, the shop or the people. A roofing contractor may have hundreds of job photos on a phone, but none picked out.
  • A list of services that lives only in the owner's head.
  • Missing basics, like exact service areas, hours, license numbers or insurance details.
  • Waiting on a logo file that someone else made years ago.

A dentist's office might need bios and headshots for every provider. A salon needs a service list and a booking link. A restaurant needs a current menu, and menus change. Each of these is a small task, but each one is a small task that nobody has done yet.

2. Slow or scattered feedback

A designer shows you a first draft. Then the project waits. It waits for you to find a quiet hour, which might be a week from now. Then it waits again for the next round.

Scattered feedback is just as bad. One note by email, another by text, a third said out loud in the truck. Things get lost, and the designer has to guess what you meant.

3. Too many decision makers

When a business has partners, a spouse who "just wants to take a look," a marketing cousin and an office manager, every opinion adds a loop. A law firm with several partners can spend a long time on one headline because everyone wants a different one.

Nobody is wrong to care. But each extra approver adds a wait, and they often contradict each other.

4. Changing the plan midway

Adding a new service page after the design is approved. Deciding you want online booking after all. Switching the color scheme once you see it live. Each change is reasonable. Together they push the finish line back, because earlier work has to be redone.

How to keep your project moving

Most of this you can do before you ever talk to a designer.

  1. Gather your material first. Collect your logo, a handful of your best photos, your service list, your hours, your address and phone number, and any badges, licenses or reviews you want shown. Put it all in one folder.
  2. Name one decision maker. Other people can give opinions, but one person has the final say. Tell your designer who that is.
  3. Collect feedback in one place. Gather everyone's thoughts, merge them into a single list, and send it once. One tidy list beats five messy messages.
  4. Set a reply habit. Answer within a day or two, even if the answer is "I need a bit more time." A short reply keeps the project from stalling.
  5. Save wish-list items for later. Launch with what matters most. A site can grow after it is live. A new page is easy to add once the main site is out in the world.
  6. Don't write everything from scratch. If writing is the hard part, ask your designer to draft the words from a short conversation, then edit them. Editing is faster than staring at a blank page.

What to have ready for common local businesses

  • Contractors and HVAC: job photos, service areas, license and insurance details, and how people request a quote.
  • Dentists and med spas: provider bios, headshots, a list of treatments, and how new patients book.
  • Salons and restaurants: current services or menu, hours, and booking or ordering links.
  • Law firms: practice areas, attorney bios, and how a first consultation works.

What the build itself involves

It helps to know what the designer is doing while you wait. They set up the structure, lay out the pages, add your content, make sure it works on phones, connect your contact form, and test it. Then there is the launch step, where your address on the web points to the new site and a security certificate is turned on. (That certificate is what puts the padlock in the browser. If you are curious, we explain it in what is an SSL certificate.)

None of that is the slow part when the designer has what they need. It is the reason a focused studio can move quickly, and the reason a project that sits waiting on content can drag on for months.

Is a rebuild the right move?

Sometimes the fastest path is not a full rebuild at all. If your current site is mostly fine and just needs fixes, that can be quicker. We wrote about how to decide in do I need a new website or can mine be fixed. And if you are weighing the budget side, see how much a small business website costs.

Going live is not the end of the work. Hours change, services change, and software needs updates. Plan for who will look after the site once it is up. We cover that in how often you should update your website, and it is the reason we offer monthly website care alongside new builds.

Common questions

So how long does it take to build a small business website?

It varies a lot, and the biggest factor is how quickly content and feedback come in. At Avello Labs, most sites go live within a week, but that depends on the project and on having your material ready.

Does a bigger site always take longer?

Usually, yes, because there is more to write, photograph and review. But a small site with slow feedback can take longer than a bigger site with quick answers.

What is the one thing I can do to speed things up?

Get your photos, service list and business details into one folder before you start. Missing content is the most common reason projects stall.

Can I add things after the site is live?

Yes. It is usually smarter to launch with the essentials and add pages later, rather than hold up the whole project for extras.

Should I wait until I have perfect photos?

No. Good, honest photos of your real work are enough to start. You can swap in better ones later, and a live site is more useful than a perfect one that is not finished.
