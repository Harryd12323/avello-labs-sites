# Why SEO matters for local businesses
When someone nearby searches for what you do, Google decides which businesses they see first. SEO is the work of making sure yours is one of them.

In short
- Most people look a business up online before they call, visit or buy.
- Google ranks local businesses mainly on relevance, distance and prominence.
- You can't change your distance from a searcher, but you can improve the other two.
- SEO is steady, ongoing work. Results build over months, not days.

## What SEO actually is
SEO (search engine optimization) means making your website and online listings easy for search engines to understand, and worth recommending. It isn't a trick or a setting you switch on once.
For most small businesses it comes down to getting the basics right and keeping them right: accurate information, useful pages, a site that works well on phones, and a good reputation.

## How Google picks local businesses
For searches like "plumber near me" or "dentist in [your town]", Google's own guidance says local results are based mainly on three things:
- Relevance: how well your listing and website match what the person searched for.
- Distance: how far you are from the searcher, or from the place named in the search.
- Prominence: how well known and trusted your business is, based on things like reviews, links and mentions across the web.
Distance is out of your hands. Relevance and prominence are where SEO helps.

## The two places you can show up

### The map results
The listings shown with a map, often called the "local pack". These are driven largely by your Google Business Profile.

### The regular results
The list of web pages below the map. These are driven by your website and how well it answers the search.
Both matter, and they support each other. A strong website helps your profile, and your profile sends people to your website.

## What actually helps
- A complete Google Business Profile with the right categories, accurate hours, your service area, real photos and regular updates.
- The same business details everywhere. Your name, address or service area, and contact details should match on your website, your profile and directories. Mismatches make your information harder to trust.
- A clear page for each service, and for the areas you serve, written for customers rather than stuffed with keywords.
- Reviews. Ask happy customers for reviews as a habit, and reply to them, good and bad.
- A fast, mobile-friendly site. Many local searches happen on phones, and a slow or awkward page loses people before they call.
- Clear page titles and descriptions, so your listing in the results says plainly what you do and where.
- Fixing technical problems such as broken pages, pages Google can't index, and errors reported in Google Search Console.

## What SEO isn't
- It isn't a guarantee. Nobody can promise you the top spot. Anyone who does is guessing, or bending Google's rules in ways that can backfire.
- It isn't instant. Changes can take weeks to be noticed, and months to show their full effect.
- It isn't one-and-done. Competitors keep improving, and Google keeps changing.

## Why it's worth it for a small business
People who find you through search are usually looking for exactly what you offer, right now. Showing up for those searches puts you in front of customers at the moment they're ready to act.
And unlike ads, which stop working the day you stop paying, the work that improves your search visibility keeps doing its job. That's why we treat SEO as part of looking after your site every month, alongside AEO and ongoing site health.

## Common questions

How long does SEO take to work?
It depends on your market and where you're starting from. Simple fixes, like correcting your business details, can help within weeks. Meaningful ranking improvements usually take a few months of steady work.
Can you guarantee I'll be number one on Google?
No, and you should be wary of anyone who does. Google decides rankings, and they depend partly on things nobody controls, like how close a searcher is to you. What we can do is honest, consistent work on the things known to help.
Do I need a Google Business Profile?
If you serve customers in a local area, yes. It's free, and it powers your listing in Google Maps and the map results. It should be claimed, verified and kept up to date.
Is SEO a one-time project?
No. The basics can be set up once, but rankings shift as competitors improve, Google updates its systems, and your business changes. Ongoing attention keeps you from slipping back.
Should I just pay for ads instead?
Ads can bring quick, targeted visits, but they stop when the spending stops. SEO builds visibility that lasts. Many businesses use both.

# What is AEO, and why it matters
More people now ask AI tools questions like “who's a good electrician near me?” instead of scrolling through search results. AEO is about making sure those tools know your business and can recommend it.

In short
- AEO stands for answer engine optimization.
- Answer engines reply with an answer instead of a list of links: ChatGPT, Google's AI Overviews, Perplexity, Microsoft Copilot and others.
- They build those answers from information they can find, and trust, on the web.
- Good AEO is mostly good SEO, plus making your information clear and easy for machines to read.

## What's changing
Traditional search shows a list of links and lets you choose. Answer engines read many sources for you and summarize them, often naming just a few businesses.
Google now shows AI-generated summaries, called AI Overviews, above many of its results. Tools like ChatGPT can search the web and name specific businesses in their answers. For a local business, that raises a new question: when someone asks an AI for a recommendation in your area, does your name come up?

## How AI tools decide what to say
Nobody outside these companies knows exactly how each tool chooses, and they change often. But they generally lean on the same kinds of sources:
- Pages they can access and read, including your website.
- Business listings and directories, such as your Google Business Profile.
- What others say about you: reviews, local news and mentions on other sites.
- Clear, consistent facts. If your hours or services differ from one site to the next, a tool may leave them out, or get them wrong.

## What helps you show up
- Plain answers to real questions. Pages that say clearly what you do, where you work, how to get started and, if you can share it, what things cost. An FAQ written in the words your customers actually use.
- Structured data. Small pieces of code, called schema markup, that label your business name, location, hours, services and FAQs so machines don't have to guess.
- Consistent details everywhere: your website, Google Business Profile, Yelp, Facebook and industry directories.
- Letting the right crawlers in. Some sites accidentally block the bots that AI search tools use to read the web. A quick check of your site's settings can catch that.
- Reputation. Reviews and mentions on trusted local sites give AI tools more confidence to recommend you.
- Keeping things current. Outdated information is a reason for a tool to leave you out, or describe you wrongly.

## AEO and SEO work together
AEO isn't a replacement for SEO. AI answers often draw on the same pages that do well in normal search, so the foundation is the same: a fast website people can trust. AEO adds a focus on how your information reads when it's pulled out and summarized somewhere else.

An honest note. AEO is new, and it's changing quickly. Nobody can guarantee that a particular AI tool will mention your business, and answers change from one question to the next. What you can do is make your business easy to find and easy to trust, which helps wherever people look.

## Common questions

Is AEO different from SEO?
They overlap a lot. SEO focuses on ranking in search results. AEO focuses on being understood and mentioned by tools that write answers. The same foundations help both, so we treat them as one ongoing job.
Can you guarantee ChatGPT will recommend my business?
No. These tools decide their own answers, and those answers change. We focus on what makes a mention more likely: clear information, consistent details, structured data and a good reputation.
How do AI tools know about my business in the first place?
Mostly from the web: your website, your business listings, reviews, and other sites that mention you. Some tools search the web at the moment you ask; others also rely on information they learned earlier.
Do I need special code on my website?
Not special, but helpful. Schema markup is a standard way to describe your business in code, and search engines already use it. It's invisible to visitors and can be added to most websites.
How can I tell whether AI tools mention my business?
Ask them the questions your customers would ask, like “best [your service] in [your town]”. Results vary by tool, wording and location, so it's worth trying several and checking again every so often.

# Ongoing site health: what it is and why it matters
A website isn't a sign you put up once. It runs on software that needs updates, and it describes a business that keeps changing. Ongoing care keeps it working and accurate.

In short
- Websites tend to break quietly: a form stops sending, a page goes missing, a certificate expires.
- Most problems are cheap to fix early and expensive to discover late.
- Care covers the technical side (updates, security, speed, backups) and the content side (hours, services, prices, photos).
- It also keeps up the foundations that SEO and AEO depend on.

## What goes wrong when nobody's watching
- Contact forms stop working, often without any error, so messages simply never arrive.
- Software and plugins fall out of date and become security risks.
- The security certificate or domain name lapses, and browsers warn visitors away.
- Pages break, or links point nowhere, after small changes.
- The site slows down as images and add-ons pile up.
- Information goes stale: old hours, discontinued services, last year's prices, a holiday closure still showing in March.
None of these announce themselves. You usually find out when a customer mentions it, or when they don't call at all.

## What ongoing care includes

### Technical health
- Software, theme and plugin updates
- Uptime monitoring, so outages are caught fast
- Regular backups that can be restored
- Speed, especially on phones
- Domain and certificate renewals

### Search and content health
- Test messages through every form
- Google Search Console errors and warnings
- Broken links and missing pages
- Up-to-date hours, services, prices and photos
- Accessibility for every visitor
On speed: Google measures how real visitors experience loading, responsiveness and layout stability with a set of metrics called Core Web Vitals. They're a useful, concrete way to tell whether a site feels fast or frustrating.

## Why it matters for a small business
Your website is often the first impression a customer gets. A broken form or outdated hours costs you customers you never hear about. Search engines and AI tools favor sites that are fast and accurate, so neglect slowly costs you visibility too.
And repairing a hacked or broken site after the fact usually takes far more time and money than keeping it healthy in the first place.

## What a month of care can look like
- Check that the site is up, fast and secure
- Apply updates and confirm nothing broke
- Send a test message through every form
- Review Search Console for new errors
- Update anything that changed: hours, services, offers, photos
- Flag anything that needs your decision
Site care goes hand in hand with SEO and AEO: a slow or broken site is hard to rank or recommend, however good everything else is.

## Common questions

My site is new. Does it still need care?
Yes. New sites still need updates, monitoring and backups, and the first few months are when you'll most want to adjust your content based on what customers ask.
My site is on Wix or Squarespace. Isn't that handled for me?
Those platforms handle hosting, security and software updates, which is a real advantage. They don't keep your content current, check that your forms reach you, fix SEO issues, or watch how you show up in search. That part is still up to you.
What happens if my site goes down?
With monitoring in place, you find out quickly instead of from a customer. The cause can be tracked down and fixed, and recent backups mean the site can be restored if needed.
Do I need to give someone access to my website?
Usually, yes, at the level needed to make updates. We recommend that you always own your domain, hosting and website accounts in your own name, and add anyone helping you as a separate user, so you're never locked out.
How is this different from SEO?
Site care keeps the website itself healthy. SEO and AEO work on how you're found. They depend on each other, because a slow or broken site is hard to rank.

# Custom web apps and tools for your business
A website tells people about your business. A web app helps you run it: a tool your customers or your team use in the browser, built around the way you already work.

In short
- A web app is software that runs in a web browser, with nothing to install.
- Websites are mostly for reading. Web apps are for doing: booking, tracking, updating, looking things up.
- A small, focused tool can replace the spreadsheet, paper form or email chain a task lives in today.
- Start small. One well-built tool that solves one real problem beats a big system nobody uses.

## Website or web app?

### A website
Pages people read: your services, hours, photos and contact details. It's mostly the same for every visitor.

### A web app
Something people use. It stores information, shows different things to different people, and lets them get something done, like checking an order or booking a slot.
Many businesses need both, and they live happily together: your website at your usual address, with a link to a customer portal or booking tool alongside it.

## Real examples

### Customer portals
A private page where customers can see their project status, documents, invoices or appointment history, instead of calling or emailing to ask.

### Order and job status trackers
A simple page, or a link sent by text, that shows where an order, repair or job stands: received, in progress, ready for pickup.

### Booking and intake forms
Appointment requests or new-client intake that ask for exactly what you need, check it as it's typed, and land in one organized list instead of your inbox.

### Inventory and photo catalogs
A searchable list of products, parts, rentals or past projects, with photos, that your team or your customers can browse, and that staff can update from a phone.

### Internal dashboards
One screen with the things you check every week, like open jobs, upcoming appointments and outstanding quotes, pulled together from wherever they live now.

### AI helpers
Assistants that answer common customer questions using your own information, or read incoming inquiries and sort them by type or urgency so the right person sees them first. They work best on clear, narrow jobs, with a person checking the results and an easy way to reach a human.

## Why businesses are moving off spreadsheets and paper
Spreadsheets and paper are flexible and familiar, and for a long time they were the only affordable option. As a business grows, they start to strain:
- Several people edit the same file, and versions drift apart.
- Information gets typed twice, once on paper and again into a computer, and mistakes creep in.
- Customers can't check anything without calling you.
- It's hard to see at a glance what's late or what needs attention.
- Important details live in one person's head, inbox or notebook, which is a problem when they're away.
Small custom tools have also become cheaper to build and run. Hosting costs little, and a focused tool doesn't need a big team behind it. That puts software built around your own process within reach of businesses that used to make do.

## When a custom tool makes sense, and when it doesn't

### Often a good fit
- You repeat the same task every day or week, and it's slow or error-prone
- Customers keep asking the same status questions
- Information is scattered across paper, spreadsheets and email
- Off-the-shelf software forces a process that doesn't fit you

### Maybe not yet
- A reasonably priced existing app already does the job well. Use it.
- The process itself isn't settled. Work it out on paper first.
- It's a one-off task that won't come up again

## What a good small tool looks like
- It does one job well, and it's obvious how to use it
- It works on a phone, because that's where people will use it
- Your data can always be exported, so it's never trapped
- It has proper logins, backups and security from day one
- It's simple to change as your business changes

## Common questions

What's the difference between a website and a web app?
A website is mostly for reading information. A web app is for getting something done: it stores information, shows different things to different people, and lets them take actions like booking or updating a record. Both run in a web browser.
Do my customers or staff need to install anything?
No. Web apps run in the browser on phones, tablets and computers. Many can also be added to a phone's home screen, so they open like a regular app.
Will a custom tool replace my spreadsheets completely?
Not necessarily. Often it replaces the part that causes problems, like intake or status updates, while spreadsheets stay useful for reports and exports.
Is an AI helper safe to use with customers?
It can be, when it has a narrow job, answers only from information you provide, and makes it easy to reach a person. It should be tested carefully and checked regularly, because AI tools can make mistakes.
How long does a custom tool take to build?
It depends entirely on what it needs to do. The best approach is to scope a small first version, agree on it in writing, and add to it once you see how it's actually used.
